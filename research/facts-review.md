## Trade routes and how they spread the plague (11)

- **TR-01 Caffa, a Genoese port** — Caffa (today Feodosiya, in Crimea) was a trading port on the Black Sea founded by the Italian city of Genoa in 1266. _[wheelis, brit-black-death]_
- **TR-02 The siege of Caffa** — A Mongol army of the Golden Horde under Khan Janibeg besieged Caffa. In autumn 1346 plague broke out among the besiegers and spread into the town. In spring the Italian merchants fled on their ships, and the disease sailed with them. _[benedictow, brit-black-death]_
- **TR-03 Corpses over the walls?** ⚖ — The notary Gabriele de' Mussi wrote that the attackers catapulted plague corpses into Caffa. A modern scientist finds this plausible, but concludes the siege was probably only one of several ways the plague left the Black Sea region, since ships and caravans from other ports carried it too. _[wheelis, brit-black-death]_
- **TR-04 The great trade network** — Large merchant ships linked Venice and Genoa with Constantinople, the Crimea, Alexandria, Tunis, London, and Bruges. At London and Bruges the Italian trade system connected to the ships (cogs) of the German Hanseatic League, which sailed the North Sea and the Baltic. _[benedictow]_
- **TR-05 Faster by sea** — Ships averaged about 40 km a day, so plague could jump 600 km by sea in two weeks. Over land it spread far more slowly: up to about 2 km a day on the busiest roads. _[benedictow]_
- **TR-06 Twelve galleys at Messina** — In early October 1347, twelve Genoese galleys arriving from the east brought the plague into the harbor of Messina in Sicily. _[piazza, brit-italy]_
- **TR-07 Ships from Constantinople** — Italian ships from Caffa reached Constantinople in May 1347 with plague on board; the epidemic broke out there in early July. Ships from Constantinople then carried it to Alexandria and to Mediterranean ports. _[benedictow]_
- **TR-08 A ship to England** ⚖ — A ship brought the plague to Melcombe Regis (part of Weymouth) in Dorset in 1348, and from there it spread across England. Sources disagree on the details: one historian says the ship came from Bordeaux and the outbreak began in June; Britannica says a ship from Calais arrived in August. _[benedictow, brit-black-death]_
- **TR-09 Hanseatic ships carry it north** — Plague reached Bergen in Norway in early July 1349 on a ship from England. Hanseatic ships heading home from Norway then carried it to Baltic and North Sea trading cities in 1349–1350. _[benedictow]_
- **TR-10 Russia last, from the west** — The plague did not reach Russia directly from the east. It arrived from the west: it entered the lands of Novgorod in late 1351, broke out in Novgorod itself in August 1352, and ravaged Moscow in 1353. _[benedictow]_
- **TR-11 Trade slumps, briefly** — Right after the Black Death, trade slumped suddenly, but the slump did not last long. _[brit-black-death]_

## Timeline of the plague, 1347–1353 (12)

- **TL-01 Where it began** — Ancient DNA from graves near Lake Issyk-Kul in Kyrgyzstan, whose tombstones mention a "pestilence" in 1338–1339, contains the plague bacterium. Scientists identified it as the ancestor of the strains behind the Black Death, pointing to a source in central Asia. _[spyrou]_
- **TL-02 The march across Europe** — From the Black Sea, the plague reached Sicily in 1347; North Africa, mainland Italy, Spain, and France in 1348; Austria, Hungary, Switzerland, Germany, and the Low Countries in 1349; and Scandinavia and the Baltic in 1350. _[brit-black-death]_
- **TL-03 Autumn 1347 in the Mediterranean** — Plague reached Alexandria around 1 September 1347 and Marseille around the second week of September 1347, both on ships from Constantinople. _[benedictow]_
- **TL-04 Genoa, Pisa and Venice** ⚖ — Britannica dates the plague's arrival to January 1348 for Genoa and Pisa and February 1348 for Venice. Historian Ole Benedictow places Genoa and Venice slightly earlier, in November 1347. _[brit-italy, benedictow]_
- **TL-05 Avignon, January 1348** — The papal physician Guy de Chauliac wrote that the great mortality began in Avignon in January 1348 and lasted seven months. _[chauliac]_
- **TL-06 Spring 1348 in France and Spain** — From Marseille the plague moved up the Rhône valley and along the coast toward Spain. By March 1348 Spain's Mediterranean coast was under attack, and by the end of March Bordeaux had become a new center of spread. _[benedictow]_
- **TL-07 Paris, summer 1348** — The plague reached Paris in the summer of 1348. The chronicler Jean de Venette dated its arrival to around August 1348, and it lasted through that year and the next. _[venette, benedictow]_
- **TL-08 London** — London was infected by about August 1348, and suffered worst between February and May 1349. _[benedictow, brit-black-death]_
- **TL-09 Winter slowed the plague** — The plague spread mostly in warm seasons. Winter slowed it or stopped it for a while, especially in the Alps and northern Europe. _[benedictow]_
- **TL-10 It came back** — The Black Death was not the end. Plague returned in 1361–63, 1369–71, 1374–75, 1390, and 1400. _[brit-black-death]_
- **TL-11 Almost nowhere escaped** — Iceland and Finland are the only regions known for certain to have escaped the Black Death, because of their small populations and limited contact with other places. _[benedictow]_
- **TL-12 The name came later** — People at the time called it "the pestilence" or "the great mortality." The name "Black Death" came centuries later, probably from a mistranslation of the Latin word atra, which can mean both "terrible" and "black." _[benedictow, chauliac]_

## Major affected cities and regions (14)

- **CI-01 Florence** — Florence was one of the hardest-hit cities. Historian Ole Benedictow estimates that 60 percent of its people died within a few months of 1348. Boccaccio set his famous book the Decameron in plague-stricken Florence. _[benedictow, boccaccio]_
- **CI-02 Chroniclers' numbers disagree** ⚖ — Writers of the time gave very different death tolls for Florence: the chronicler Villani said three in every five died, Archbishop Antoninus estimated 60,000, and Boccaccio said 100,000. Numbers like these are rough guesses. _[brown-decameron]_
- **CI-03 A chronicler dies** — Giovanni Villani, a Florentine chronicler who had been a partner in the Peruzzi banking company, died in the plague of 1348. _[brit-villani]_
- **CI-04 Siena's unfinished cathedral** — Siena lost so many people that its plan to enlarge the cathedral was abandoned, and the deaths of painters such as Ambrogio and Pietro Lorenzetti ended the first Sienese school of painting. _[brit-black-death]_
- **CI-05 Paris: the Hôtel-Dieu** — In Paris, Jean de Venette wrote that more than 500 dead a day were carried from the Hôtel-Dieu hospital to the cemetery, and that many of the nuns who nursed the sick died too. _[venette]_
- **CI-06 Uneven death rates** — Death rates varied from place to place. The duchy of Milan, Flanders, and Béarn seem to have escaped comparatively lightly, while Tuscany, Aragon, Catalonia, and Languedoc were very hard-hit. _[brit-black-death]_
- **CI-07 A princess at Bordeaux** — Even royalty died. Joan, daughter of King Edward III of England, died at Bordeaux on her way to marry the son of the king of Castile. _[brit-black-death]_
- **CI-08 Tunis and Ibn Khaldun** — The plague struck Tunis in North Africa and killed both parents of the young scholar Ibn Khaldun, who later became a famous historian. _[brit-ibn-khaldun, brit-black-death]_
- **CI-09 Ragusa nearly emptied** — In 1348 the Black Death nearly emptied Ragusa (today Dubrovnik, Croatia), a merchant republic on the Adriatic that depended on grain brought in by ship. _[brit-ragusa]_
- **CI-10 England loses half** — In 1348–49 the Black Death killed around half of England's population. About 1,000 English villages were emptied or disappeared completely. _[bailey, brit-black-death]_
- **CI-11 Messina flees** — In Messina the Genoese sailors were driven out, but the disease spread anyway. Terrified people fled to nearby Catania, which refused to let them be buried inside the town. _[piazza]_
- **CI-12 The Low Countries were not spared** ⚖ — Some older maps show the Low Countries (including Flanders and Bruges) as almost untouched by plague. Newer research shows this came from gaps in the old data: many regions of the Low Countries were hit by the Black Death. _[roosen-curtis, brit-black-death]_
- **CI-13 Strasbourg, summer 1349** — The chronicler Jacob von Königshofen recorded that the plague reached Strasbourg in the summer of 1349, months after the city's Jewish community had been murdered. He estimated that about 16,000 people died there. _[fordham-jews]_
- **DB-01 How many died?** ⚖ — Estimates differ. Britannica gives about 25 million deaths in Europe, around one-third of the population, which matches what the chronicler Froissart said at the time. Historian Ole Benedictow argues for about 60 percent, around 50 million. Regional death rates ranged from one-eighth to two-thirds. _[brit-black-death, benedictow]_

## Social responses (13)

- **SO-01 A punishment from God?** — Most people believed the plague was God's punishment for their sins. They answered with prayer, processions, and acts of penance, or with fatalism. _[benedictow, carlin-paris]_
- **SO-02 The flagellants** — Flagellants marched from town to town in processions with crosses and banners, whipping themselves to atone for sins and calling on people to repent. Their movement grew quickly during the Black Death. Members vowed to take part for 33 and a half days. _[brit-flagellants, toke-flagellants, venette]_
- **SO-03 The Pope bans the flagellants** — Pope Clement VI condemned the flagellant movement and banned its public processions in letters dated 20 October 1349. After that the movement shrank quickly. _[toke-flagellants, venette]_
- **SO-04 Flight from the cities** — Many people who could afford it fled the cities for the countryside. Boccaccio's Decameron begins with a group of young Florentines leaving the city, and the physician Guy de Chauliac wrote that the best protection was to flee before the plague arrived. _[boccaccio, chauliac]_
- **SO-05 Shutting themselves away** — Some families shut themselves inside houses where no one was sick and lived apart from everyone else. _[boccaccio]_
- **SO-06 Families torn apart** — Fear broke normal bonds. Writers described brothers abandoning brothers and even parents abandoning their children. Funerals became rushed or stopped altogether. _[boccaccio, chauliac, brown-decameron]_
- **SO-07 A false accusation** — Across Europe, Jews were falsely accused of causing the plague by poisoning wells. The accusation was untrue: Jews were dying of the plague too. Beginning in September 1348, Jews were tortured into false "confessions." _[ej-black-death, venette, chauliac]_
- **SO-08 Clement VI defends the Jews** — In 1348 Pope Clement VI issued papal bulls (official letters) that rejected the poisoning accusation and called for Jews to be protected. He also welcomed Jews in Avignon. Elsewhere his appeals had little effect, and the massacres continued. _[ej-bulls, ej-black-death, brit-clement]_
- **SO-09 Strasbourg, February 1349** — On 14 February 1349, before the plague had even reached Strasbourg, the city's Jewish community was murdered. The city council, which had refused to act against the Jews, was overthrown first. The chronicler Königshofen admitted that debts owed to Jewish moneylenders were a real motive. _[fordham-jews, ej-black-death]_
- **SO-10 Some tried to protect** — Some leaders tried to protect Jewish communities, such as the old city council of Strasbourg and the leading citizens of Basel, but mobs overruled them. _[ej-black-death, fordham-jews]_
- **SO-11 Guards at the gates** — Towns posted guards at their gates and turned away strangers. Pistoia in Tuscany banned its people from traveling to or from nearby Pisa and Lucca. _[chauliac, douglas]_
- **SO-12 Life after the plague** — After the epidemic ended, the survivors married, and Jean de Venette noticed an unusually large number of births. _[venette]_
- **SO-13 A less rigid society** — With so many workers dead, the survivors gained bargaining power. This brought a new flexibility to what had been a very rigid social order. _[brit-black-death]_

## Economic consequences (9)

- **EC-01 Troubled times before the plague** — Italy's economy was already struggling before the plague arrived. The great Florentine banks failed: the Peruzzi in 1343, the Acciaiuoli in 1345, and the Bardi in 1346. There were also famines in 1346–47. _[brit-italy]_
- **EC-02 Too few workers** — So many laborers died that much less land could be farmed, which ruined many landowners. To keep tenants, lords began paying wages or charging money rents instead of demanding unpaid labor. _[brit-black-death]_
- **EC-03 Wages rise** — Because workers were scarce, wages rose for both craftsmen and peasants. _[brit-black-death, brown-decameron]_
- **EC-04 Everything twice as dear** — Prices rose too. Jean de Venette complained that after the plague everything cost twice as much: tools, food, goods, and hired workers. _[venette, brown-decameron]_
- **EC-05 The Ordinance of Labourers (1349)** — On 18 June 1349, while the plague was still raging, the English Crown issued the Ordinance of Labourers. It capped wages at pre-plague levels and required able-bodied people under 60 to work. _[bailey, statute-avalon]_
- **EC-06 The Statute of Labourers (1351)** — In February 1351, at the first Parliament after the plague, the Statute of Labourers strengthened these rules. It complained that workers refused to work unless paid two or three times the old wages. _[bailey, statute-avalon]_
- **EC-07 The wage laws failed** — The labor laws soon failed to hold wages down. They caused lasting resentment, which fed into the grievances behind the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. _[bailey, cannon]_
- **EC-08 A long recovery** — Western Europe's population did not return to its pre-1348 level until the early 1500s. _[brit-black-death]_
- **EC-09 Wars paused** — For a short time the plague brought a pause in wars. _[brit-black-death]_

## Medical understanding and treatments (15)

- **ME-01 The real cause** — Today we know the Black Death was plague, caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. DNA taken from medieval skeletons has confirmed this. No one at the time knew bacteria existed. _[brit-black-death, spyrou, benedictow]_
- **ME-02 Bubonic and pneumonic plague** — Bubonic plague causes painful swellings called buboes in the groin, armpits, or neck. If the infection reaches the lungs it becomes pneumonic plague, a more severe form that can spread through coughing. _[brit-bubonic, benedictow]_
- **ME-03 Chauliac's two forms** — In Avignon, Guy de Chauliac described two forms. For the first two months, victims had fever and coughed up blood, and died within three days. After that, victims had swellings in the armpits and groin and died within five days. _[chauliac]_
- **ME-04 Blamed on the planets** — In October 1348 the medical faculty of the University of Paris reported that the plague was caused by a conjunction of three planets on 20 March 1345, which had supposedly corrupted the air. _[carlin-paris]_
- **ME-05 Bad air and the humors** — Medieval doctors believed illness came from "corrupt air" (the miasma theory) and from an imbalance of the body's four humors. Guy de Chauliac wrote that the planets had turned the body's humors "venomous." _[carlin-paris, chauliac]_
- **ME-06 Treatments of the time** — Doctors tried bloodletting, purging pills of aloe, fires to "purify" the air, a medicine called theriac, sweet-smelling herbs, and poultices of cooked figs and onions on the swellings. None of these could cure plague. _[chauliac, brit-bubonic]_
- **ME-07 Flowers and spices** — People in Florence walked around holding flowers, fragrant herbs, or spices to their noses, hoping good smells would protect them from bad air. _[boccaccio]_
- **ME-08 Doctors were helpless** — Both Boccaccio and the physician Guy de Chauliac admitted that medicine could do almost nothing against the plague. Today plague is treated with antibiotics. _[boccaccio, chauliac, brit-bubonic]_
- **ME-09 A doctor who stayed** — Guy de Chauliac stayed in Avignon to avoid a bad reputation. Near the end of the outbreak he caught the plague himself, was ill for about six weeks, and survived. _[chauliac]_
- **ME-10 Rats or people?** ⚖ — Historians still debate how plague spread from person to person. The traditional view blames fleas on black rats. A 2018 study found that human fleas and body lice fit the death records of nine European outbreaks better. _[benedictow, dean]_
- **ME-11 No beaked masks yet** — The famous beaked "plague doctor" costume did not exist during the Black Death. It was invented by the French physician Charles de Lorme in the 1600s, almost 300 years later. _[wellcome-mask, natgeo-mask]_
- **ME-12 Early health measures** — During the Black Death some Italian cities tried early health measures. By 1348 Venice required incoming ships to wait before sailors could enter the city, and in Milan the ruler Luchino Visconti ordered houses with sick people boarded up. These measures were haphazard and limited. _[douglas]_
- **ME-13 No plan in England** — England had no coordinated public-health response to the Black Death, not even restrictions on travel or isolation of the sick. The government's main concern was keeping order. _[bailey]_
- **ME-14 What came next: quarantine (1377)** — Formal quarantine came later. On 27 July 1377, after the plague had kept returning, Ragusa ordered travelers from plague areas to spend 30 days in isolation on the island of Mrkan or in the town of Cavtat before entering. _[brit-ragusa, gensini]_
- **ME-15 What came next: forty days** — The 30-day wait was later extended to 40 days, which Venice also adopted. Our word "quarantine" comes from quaranta, Italian for forty. Venice opened the first permanent plague hospital (lazaretto) in 1423. _[brit-ragusa, tran, tognotti, gensini]_

## The Catholic Church and religious practice (8)

- **CH-01 Popes at Avignon** — During the Black Death the popes lived in Avignon, not Rome (1309–1377). Clement VI was pope from 1342 until his death in Avignon in 1352. _[brit-clement]_
- **CH-02 The papal court loses a quarter** — About one-fourth of Pope Clement VI's staff at Avignon died in the Black Death. _[brit-clement, brit-black-death]_
- **CH-03 Clergy hit hard** — Priests caught the disease while caring for the sick and dying. In English dioceses about 45–50 percent of the clergy died, and monasteries suffered some of the highest death rates of all. _[newberry, brit-black-death]_
- **CH-04 Two archbishops in a row** — Canterbury, England's most important church post, lost two archbishops one after the other: John de Stratford and Thomas Bradwardine. _[brit-black-death]_
- **CH-05 Confess to anyone** — So many priests died that in 1349 Ralph of Shrewsbury, Bishop of Bath and Wells, told his people that if no priest could be found, the dying could confess their sins to any layperson, "even to a woman." _[newberry]_
- **CH-06 Faith shaken** — Faith in the Church weakened, partly because so many clergy had died. The Church lost some of its monopoly over saving souls as people turned to mysticism. North of the Alps, art and poetry became preoccupied with death. _[brit-black-death, brown-decameron]_
- **CH-07 Religion at home** — After the Black Death, more ordinary people practiced religion on their own, using prayer books such as Books of Hours and asking for religious writings in their own language instead of Latin. Criticism of clergy also grew. _[newberry]_
- **CH-08 The Jubilee of 1350** ⚖ — In 1343 Clement VI had declared a Holy Year (Jubilee) for 1350. Despite the plague and an earthquake in Rome in 1349, huge crowds of pilgrims came to Rome. The Vatican reports more than one and a half million. _[vatican-jubilee]_
