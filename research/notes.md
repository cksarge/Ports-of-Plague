# Research notes (raw quotes gathered while verifying facts)

## S-BRIT-BD — Britannica, "Black Death" (Britannica Editors; rev. Amy Tikkanen) https://www.britannica.com/event/Black-Death  (accessed 2026-09-25 via browser)
- "pandemic that ravaged Europe between 1347 and 1351 ... It is estimated that 25 million people died."
- "widely believed to have been the result of plague, caused by infection with the bacterium Yersinia pestis. Modern genetic analyses indicate that the strain ... is ancestral to all extant circulating Y. pestis strains"
- "Having originated in China and Inner Asia, the Black Death decimated the army of the Kipchak khan Janibeg while he was besieging the Genoese trading port of Kaffa (now Feodosiya) in Crimea (1347). ... Janibeg used trebuchets to catapult plague-infested corpses into the town"
- "From Kaffa, Genoese ships carried the epidemic westward to Mediterranean ports, whence it spread inland, affecting Sicily (1347); North Africa, mainland Italy, Spain, and France (1348); and Austria, Hungary, Switzerland, Germany, and the Low Countries (1349)."
- "A ship from Calais carried the plague to Melcombe Regis, Dorset, in August 1348. It reached Bristol almost immediately ... London suffered most violently between February and May 1349"
- "reached the extreme north of England, Scotland, Scandinavia, and the Baltic countries in 1350."
- recurrences 1361–63, 1369–71, 1374–75, 1390, 1400
- Mortality varied: Milan, Flanders, Béarn escaped comparatively lightly; Tuscany, Aragon, Catalonia, Languedoc very hard-hit. Towns worse than countryside; monastic communities highest incidence.
- Joan, daughter of Edward III, died at Bordeaux on way to wedding. Canterbury lost two successive archbishops. "The papal court at Avignon was reduced by one-fourth."
## Britannica "Effects and significance"
- "A cessation of wars and a sudden slump in trade immediately followed but were only of short duration."
- "The shortage of labor compelled them to substitute wages or money rents in place of labor services ... There was also a general rise in wages for artisans and peasants."
- "the Roman Catholic Church lost some of its monopoly over the salvation of souls as people turned to mysticism"
- "Antisemitism greatly intensified throughout Europe as Jews were blamed for the spread ... A wave of violent pogroms ensued"
- Siena cathedral enlargement abandoned; Lorenzetti brothers died.
- "mortality varying in the different regions between one-eighth and two-thirds of the population ... Froissart's statement that about one-third ... may be fairly accurate." England: ~1,000 villages depopulated/disappeared. W. Europe population did not regain pre-1348 level until beginning of 16th c.

## S-BENED — Ole J. Benedictow, "The Black Death: The Greatest Catastrophe Ever," History Today 55.3 (March 2005). https://www.historytoday.com/archive/black-death-greatest-catastrophe-ever
- Spread across Europe 1346-53. Name "Black Death" came centuries later; probably mistranslation of Latin 'atra' (terrible/black).
- 60% of Florence's population died in a few months; probably same in Siena. Agnolo di Tura: "I ... buried my five children with my own hands". Petrarch lost Laura.
- bubonic plague, Y. pestis, black rats; "It now appears clear that human fleas and lice did not contribute to the spread, at least not significantly." (Benedictow's view — contrast Dean et al. 2018)
- pneumonic plague: "not contracted easily, spreads normally only episodically"
- plague a disease of warmer seasons; pace slowed in winter.
- Origin: "began in the spring of 1346 in the steppe region ... north-western shores of the Caspian Sea into southern Russia" (Golden Horde)
- Kaffa: "In the autumn of 1346, plague broke out among the besiegers and from them penetrated into the town. When spring arrived, the Italians fled on their ships."
- Trade networks "linked Venice and Genoa with Constantinople and the Crimea, Alexandria and Tunis, London and Bruges. In London and Bruges the Italian trading system was linked to ... the German Hanseatic League ... cogs."
- "Most people believed plague and mass illness to be a punishment from God for their sins."
- Ships ~40 km/day -> 600 km in a fortnight; by land up to 2 km/day on busiest roads, ~0.6 km/day secondary.
- "Italian ships from Kaffa arrived in Constantinople in May 1347 ... epidemic broke loose in early July." Alexandria ~1 September 1347 via ship from Constantinople. Marseilles ~second week of September 1347. Genoa and Venice "some time in November" 1347. Ships from Genoa also contaminated Pisa.
- March 1348: Lyon's and Spain's Mediterranean coasts under attack. Bordeaux critical centre by end of March 1348. Rouen end of April 1348; June: front toward Paris and Low Countries.
- Ship from Bordeaux arrived ~May 8 in Melcombe Regis; outbreak shortly before June 24 1348. Bristol June; London contaminated early August 1348 (outbreak comment end of Sept).
- Oslo autumn 1348 (from SE England). Bergen early July 1349, ship from England probably King's Lynn. Hanseatic ships spread it; Elbing outbreak 24 Aug 1349; other Hanseatic cities autumn 1349; northern German front spring 1350. Denmark/Sweden mostly ravaged by end of 1350.
- Novgorod territory late autumn 1351; Pskov; full outbreak spring 1352; Novgorod itself mid-August (1352). 1353 Moscow ravaged.
- Iceland and Finland only regions known with certainty to have avoided it.
- Earlier estimates 20-30%; Benedictow: ~60% of Europe's population, ~50 million of ~80 million.

## S-WHEELIS — Wheelis, Mark. "Biological Warfare at the 1346 Siege of Caffa." Emerging Infectious Diseases 8.9 (2002): 971-975. https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/8/9/01-0536_article
- Caffa established by Genoa in 1266. Janibeg renewed siege 1345, lifted after a year due to plague.
- de' Mussi (Piacenza notary) claims plague transmitted by hurling diseased cadavers into the city. Wheelis: plausible means of transmission into the city.
- "Some boats were bound for Genoa, others went to Venice and to other Christian areas." (de' Mussi)
- "refugees from Caffa would most likely have constituted only one of several streams of infected ships and caravans leaving the region." Overland caravan routes also.
- Conclusion: "The siege of Caffa ... probably had no more than anecdotal importance in the spread of plague"

## S-SPYROU — Spyrou, Maria A., et al. "The source of the Black Death in fourteenth-century central Eurasia." Nature 606 (2022): 718-724. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04800-3 (PMC9217749)
- ancient DNA from 7 individuals, Kara-Djigach and Burana cemeteries (Lake Issyk-Kul, Kyrgyzstan), tombstones dated 1338-1339 mentioning pestilence; Y. pestis involvement; strain = most recent common ancestor of the diversification associated with pandemic's emergence.

## S-DEAN — Dean, Katharine R., et al. "Human ectoparasites and the spread of plague in Europe during the Second Pandemic." PNAS 115.6 (2018): 1304-1309. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1715640115
- "While it is commonly assumed that rats and their fleas spread plague during the Second Pandemic, there is little historical and archaeological support for such a claim. Here, we show that human ectoparasites, like body lice and human fleas, might be more likely than rats..."

## S-STATUTE — "The Statute of Laborers; 1351." Avalon Project, Yale Law School (from Henderson, Select Historical Documents of the Middle Ages, 1896). https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/statlab.asp
- "Because a great part of the people and especially of the workmen and servants has now died in that pestilence, some, seeing the straights of the masters and the scarcity of servants, are not willing to serve unless they receive excessive wages"
- wages as "in the twentieth year of our reign" (1346-47) or five or six years preceding; applies to able-bodied under sixty; refusal -> imprisonment.

## S-BAILEY — Bailey, Mark. "The implementation of national labour legislation in England after the Black Death, 1349–1400." Economic History Review (first published online 24 May 2024). https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13355 (open access)
- Ordinance of Labourers issued 18 June 1349; Statute of Labourers at first post-plague parliament Feb 1351.
- laws capped wages, imposed annual contracts, restricted mobility; "despite subsequent revisions, the legislation soon failed."
- "In 1348‒9, the Black Death swept through England and killed around half its population." returned 1361-2 (~15%).
- "There was no coordinated public health response to the initial epidemics in England, not even basic restrictions on the movement of people or the isolation of the sick"
- statute intro: workers "unwilling to work unless they were paid twice or three times the level of wages before the pestilence."

## S-CANNON — Cannon, John. "Labourers, Statute of." The Oxford Companion to British History (via Encyclopedia.com). https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/labourers-statute

## S-BENED (again) — Benedictow, History Today
- "The plague must have arrived in Oslo in the autumn of 1348, and must have come with a ship from south-eastern England"; "The outbreak in Oslo was soon stopped by the advent of winter weather, but it broke out again in the early spring."
- "Hanseatic ships fleeing homewards from their trading station in Oslo"; "The outbreak of the Black Death in the Prussian town of Elbing (today the Polish town of Elblag) on August 24th, 1349"
- "It entered the territory of the city state of Novgorod in the late autumn of 1351 and reached the town of Pskov just before the winter set in and temporarily suppressed the epidemic; thus the full outbreak did not start until the early spring of 1352."
